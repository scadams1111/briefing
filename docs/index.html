Kinch argues domestic research cuts, not Chinese competition, threaten U.S. biotech leadership

“The data suggests our biggest risk isn't Chinese capital, but the fact that our domestic research output has been flat since 2018 while their public sector is scaling rapidly.”

1U.S. drug discovery output has stagnated since 2018 while China’s public sector now outproduces American counterparts.

Kinch and colleagues at the Center for Research Innovation in Biotechnology tracked 17,000 new drugs entering human trials between 2000 and 2024. They found that while overall numbers converged, the composition differs significantly. Chinese sponsors have taken a commanding lead in certain monoclonal antibody categories, such as multispecific antibodies and drug conjugates, and often introduced more novel cell therapies than American sponsors by the late 2010s. In 2024, China’s public sector, including universities and state-linked enterprises, contributed more than twice as many new drugs as their American counterparts. This surge contrasts with the flatlining of U.S. contributions since 2018, suggesting that the primary threat to U.S. leadership is the erosion of the domestic research enterprise rather than foreign capital inflows.

2Recent U.S. and Chinese proposals restrict investment but leave licensing open, creating a division of labor rather than a full decoupling.

Beijing proposed barring foreign acquisition of proprietary cell and gene platforms, while the U.S. Treasury proposed restricting American equity stakes and joint ventures in Chinese biotech. However, neither proposal impacts licensing agreements. American firms can still license Chinese molecules, and Chinese companies can sell to U.S. and European partners. Kinch argues this creates a division of labor where China increasingly invents and owns assets, while American firms take licenses, complete late-stage development, and pay royalties. The author notes that foreign deals for Chinese biotech assets reached $120 billion through August 2026, up more than a third from the previous year, indicating that the open door for licensing remains more significant than the walls built around investment.

3Kinch recommends narrowing restrictions to platform transfers and prioritizing stable funding for U.S. strengths in nucleic acids and gene therapy.

The author suggests three steps: first, use the public comment period to press Treasury for narrow rules targeting platform transfers rather than all investment, with clear criteria for review. Second, policymakers should prioritize investment in areas where the U.S. still leads, such as nucleic acid drugs and gene therapy, which rely on decades of Western public and private investment in tools like lipid nanoparticles and CRISPR. Third, Washington should measure the race by drug or disease type rather than aggregate totals, tracking first-in-human trials by sponsor country to identify long-term outcomes like FDA approvals. Kinch emphasizes that stable funding for models like NIH translational research is the most reliable way to protect America’s declining margin in these critical technologies.

Iran’s war survival paradox: external conflict entrenches theocratic rule

“The counterintuitive lesson here is that external military pressure often hardens revolutionary regimes rather than breaking them, turning a crisis of legitimacy into a struggle for national survival.”

1External war hardened Iran’s regime rather than weakening it, creating a dangerous precedent for future conflicts.

The article argues that the 2026 U.S.-Israeli war, which eliminated top leaders and decimated Iran’s military, paradoxically strengthened the Islamic Republic. Instead of rallying the impoverished public, the conflict rallied the regime around revolutionary ideology. Tehran concluded that violence, not dialogue, secures concessions, establishing new precedents for attacking neighbors and disrupting global energy supplies via the Strait of Hormuz. The author notes that while Washington can end its war, it cannot end Iran’s ideological confrontation with the U.S., making the current ceasefire an intermission rather than a resolution. This dynamic suggests that future U.S. interventions may further harden Tehran’s stance, as the regime views survival through continuity of revolutionary principles rather than political reform.

2Iran’s economy has regressed significantly since 1979, with real GDP per capita a quarter below its 1976 peak.

Despite abundant natural resources, Iran’s theocratic rule has led to a case study in squandered national potential. The article highlights that Iran’s real GDP per capita remains nearly 25% below its 1976 level, meaning many citizens are materially worse off than their pre-revolution ancestors. This economic decline contrasts sharply with social progress; the population has tripled, becoming more urban, educated, and socially liberal. The breaking of the mandatory veiling taboo post-2022 signals a society moving beyond the revolution’s constraints, while rulers look backward. This widening gap between a progressive populace and a rigid, crisis-dependent elite creates a volatile equilibrium where the regime relies on continued brutality to suppress the conditions that produce unrest.

3Revolutionary regimes are statistically more resilient than non-revolutionary ones, relying on cohesive elites and loyal coercive apparatuses.

Citing political scientists Steven Levitsky and Lucan Way, the article notes that revolutionary regimes last nearly three times as long as their counterparts, despite being twice as likely to engage in war. The Islamic Republic’s durability stems from three pillars: a cohesive ruling elite, a loyal coercive apparatus (specifically the IRGC), and the destruction of rival power centers. Crises, such as the 1979 hostage crisis and the Iran-Iraq War, served to entrench theocratic government by marginalizing moderates and forging the bonds of the current security elite. With the new, largely invisible leadership of Mojtaba Khamenei, the regime faces an unstable equilibrium, but the absence of high-level defections suggests that the entrenched veto power of the status quo remains formidable against any internal reform movements.

Ambetter’s practice contrasts with industry pledges to cut prior‑auth use by 11% across plans by 2026.

Foreign Graduate Enrollment in U.S. Biomedicine Drops Sharply Amid Immigration Restrictions

“The recent dip in international biomedical Ph.D. students threatens U.S. research leadership and biotech competitiveness.”

1International graduate enrollment in U.S. biomedical programs fell sharply in 2026

Data released by the Institute of International Education (IIE) from a survey of more than 800 U.S. institutions show that total foreign student enrollment for the 2025‑2026 academic year fell 9% compared with the prior year. The decline is steeper among new entrants, with a 15% drop in newly enrolled students and a 15% reduction in graduate‑level enrolments. A separate analysis by the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) of 37 member schools indicates that the number of international students beginning biomedical Ph.D. programs has fallen about 30% since 2024, more than double the 14% overall decline in those programs. An independent review of the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System (SEVIS) by the Presidents’ Alliance on Higher Education and Immigration reports a 6.3% drop in international student numbers between October 2025 and October 2026. Together, these figures represent the largest year‑to‑year decrease in graduate enrollment in at least two decades.

2Legal victories have not stopped the chilling effect of immigration policy on foreign scholars

Federal judges have recently blocked two of the Trump administration’s most aggressive immigration proposals. On November 16, a judge issued a final ruling that stopped a four‑year cap on the duration of status for international students and postdoctoral researchers, finding the rule violated the Administrative Procedure Act. The following day, another judge granted a preliminary injunction preventing the administration from narrowing eligibility for the Optional Practical Training (OPT) work‑authorization program, which many foreign students rely on for professional experience. Despite these victories, the administration continued to pursue policies that signal hostility toward foreign scholars, including revoking more than 8,000 student visas, proposing a $70,000 fee for OPT, and attempting to reinstate a $100,000 H‑1B fee. A Department of Homeland Security spokesperson dismissed the IIE data as “misleading,” yet independent SEVIS analysis still shows a decline. The combined effect, according to student advocates and immigration attorneys, is a pervasive sense among international scholars that they are unwelcome, which depresses enrollment regardless of court outcomes.

3U.S. risks losing global biotech talent to Asia as other nations attract foreign students

The enrollment trends are reshaping the global competition for scientific talent. While U.S. institutions report declines, surveys indicate that foreign graduate enrollment is rising at many Asian universities, with about 60% of respondents from China and other Asian countries noting increases. The United Kingdom, which has also tightened immigration rules, sees a similar drop in foreign graduate numbers. Analysts warn that the United States could lose its biomedical preeminence as students and researchers choose destinations that appear more welcoming and financially predictable. A shift of even a modest fraction of the roughly one‑quarter of life‑science Ph.D. graduates who are international could erode the pipeline of talent that fuels biotech innovation in San Diego and elsewhere. The long‑term economic impact may extend beyond academia, affecting industry R&D, venture capital investment, and the broader U.S. economy, as other nations capitalize on the talent vacuum.

Iambic's AI-designed CDK inhibitor outperforms Ibrance in preclinical cancer models

“Iambic’s recent data suggests their AI-driven 'agentic factory' isn't just a buzzword, as their new CDK inhibitor outperformed Ibrance in resistant models, potentially solving a major resistance hurdle in oncology.”

1IAM-C1, an AI-designed dual CDK2/4 inhibitor, showed superior tumor growth inhibition compared to Pfizer's Ibrance in resistant breast cancer lines.

Iambic Therapeutics presented preclinical data for IAM-C1 at the EORTC-NCI-AACR symposium, highlighting its potential to overcome resistance mechanisms common in CDK4/6 inhibitors. In a CDK4 and CDK6 inhibitor-resistant breast cancer line, IAM-C1 achieved 78% tumor growth inhibition, significantly outperforming Ibrance, which showed only 24% inhibition. The compound is a selective dual inhibitor of CDK2 and CDK4, targeting kinases that regulate cell division. CEO Tom Miller described the result as a 'holy grail molecular profile,' noting that cancer cells often use CDK2 as an escape route when CDK4 is blocked, leading to toxicity. The data also showed efficacy in ovarian and endometrial adenocarcinoma models, with the drug depleting cells in the S phase of the cell cycle. This performance suggests the AI platform can identify molecular profiles that address specific resistance pathways, a key challenge in oncology drug development.

2Iambic utilizes an automated 'agentic factory' where robots generate data to continuously train its AI, creating a scalable drug discovery loop.

The company’s platform is described as an automated, scalable chemistry and biology experimentation system. Once the AI designs a candidate, robots conduct the subsequent biology and chemistry testing. The results from these automated experiments are fed back into the AI platform to refine future designs. CEO Tom Miller characterizes this system as an 'automated agentic factory for the creation of new data,' which expands the company's 'data moat' daily. This approach aims to address the totality of drug discovery by closing the loop between computational design and physical experimentation. The system is central to Iambic's value proposition, allowing for rapid iteration and optimization of molecular candidates like IAM-C1 and IAM-217, distinguishing it from traditional, slower drug discovery pipelines.

3Iambic’s KIF18A inhibitor, IAM-217, received FDA clearance for clinical trials after showing 90% regression in brain metastasis models.

In addition to IAM-C1, Iambic presented data on IAM-217, a small molecule targeting KIF18A, a protein regulating chromosome alignment. In an intracranial breast cancer model of brain metastasis, orally administered IAM-217 entered the brain and drove nearly 90% tumor regression. The FDA has granted clearance for IAM-217 to enter clinical trials, with first-to-human dosing planned for the near term. This follows the company’s October IPO, which raised $150 million. Funds are earmarked for phase 3 studies of the lead candidate IAM-1363, a brain-penetrant HER2 inhibitor currently in phase 2, and to advance the KIF18A inhibitor into the clinic. The successful IND clearance validates the platform’s ability to generate clinically viable candidates across different oncology targets.

AI‑Driven Surge in Cyber Vulnerabilities Raises Patch‑Race Risks

“The AI‑driven acceleration of vulnerability discovery is forcing a faster, riskier patch cycle that could destabilize critical infrastructure if not managed.”

1AI models are dramatically increasing the rate of discovered software vulnerabilities.

Andrew Lohn, a senior fellow at Georgetown’s Center for Security and Emerging Technology, notes that monthly vulnerability disclosures jumped from roughly four thousand in early 2023 to about six thousand by April, then accelerated to sixteen thousand per month—a four‑fold increase. The AI‑enhanced tools can locate flaws faster than human analysts, creating a torrent of patches that defenders must apply. Each patch carries a non‑zero chance of introducing new failures, so a ten‑fold rise in patch frequency could produce ten times more outage incidents, as seen in recent disruptions at the FAA, Apple, Microsoft, and major banks. Lohn emphasizes that the race to deploy fixes often outpaces the ability to test them thoroughly, amplifying systemic fragility across critical infrastructure and commercial platforms.

2Coordinated vulnerability disclosure windows are shrinking, pressuring defenders to patch faster.

Traditional coordinated vulnerability disclosure gives vendors 45 to 90 days to remediate a reported flaw before public disclosure. Lohn explains that Anthropic recently reduced its critical‑issue window to thirty days, reflecting a belief that attackers are finding exploits more quickly. This compression forces organizations to accelerate their patch development and deployment pipelines, increasing operational stress and the likelihood of mis‑configurations. The faster disclosure also alerts potential adversaries to unpatched weaknesses, creating a feedback loop where attackers can exploit the same vulnerability before most users have applied the fix. Lohn warns that the shortened timeline may erode the defensive advantage historically provided by the disclosure lag, especially for smaller institutions with limited cybersecurity staffing.

3Economic costs and system resilience are keeping large‑scale cyber catastrophes from materializing.

Despite the heightened vulnerability discovery rate, Lohn points to several mitigating factors. Ransomware attack frequency has risen, yet average payouts have declined, suggesting that organizations are improving backups and recovery procedures. Health‑care breach reports show stable incident numbers but fewer affected individuals, indicating better containment. Moreover, the financial barrier to purchasing zero‑day exploits—often hundreds of thousands of dollars—remains prohibitive for many non‑state actors, and even if costs fall, the economic incentive to cause widespread disruption may not outweigh the risk of legal repercussions. Resilience measures, such as hardware interlocks that prevent dangerous commands from affecting physical systems, have limited the impact of attacks on water and power infrastructure. These economic and technical safeguards collectively reduce the probability of a true cyber‑apocalypse, even as AI accelerates threat discovery.

Three Books Reveal Hidden Forces Shaping Policy, Technology, and Economic Booms

“These books show how hidden infrastructure, communication tech, and historic economic booms shape policy, innovation, and the societal impacts we face today.”

1‘After the Flush’ reveals hidden environmental costs of modern sewage systems

Nick Kawa, an anthropologist, spent a decade documenting the life cycle of human waste in ‘After the Flush.’ The book blends lyrical prose with a hands‑on experiment in which a toilet seat sits over a bucket, illustrating how modern societies treat sewage as invisible. Kawa argues that the ‘flush‑and‑forget’ mindset obscures the environmental toll of treatment plants, nutrient runoff, and infrastructure strain. He links these issues to broader policy debates about water quality standards and climate‑resilient design, noting that even trivial distractions—like playing Wordle on the bathroom wall—can mask systemic neglect. By foregrounding the physics of effluent and the social habits surrounding it, the work calls for more rigorous regulation and public awareness of waste streams, a point especially relevant to anyone involved in animal‑research facilities or biotech labs that generate liquid waste.

2James Gleick’s ‘The Telephone’ traces how a single invention reshaped society and control

James Gleick’s ‘The Telephone’ offers a sweeping narrative of the device’s invention, corporate battles, and cultural ripple effects. Starting with early patent disputes, Gleick details a 19th‑century injunction that required operators to end calls with the phrase ‘That is all,’ a rule that shaped conversational etiquette. He then follows the emergence of ‘invisible interlopers’—government agencies and private firms that tapped lines, raising early privacy concerns that echo today’s digital surveillance debates. The book also chronicles how the telephone spurred new business models, urban planning, and social norms, from the rise of call‑centers to the redefinition of intimacy across distances. Gleick’s storytelling is anchored in archival material and interviews, providing concrete examples of how a single technology can become a locus of power struggles, regulatory challenges, and societal transformation, lessons that resonate with current AI‑driven communication tools.

3‘The Rush’ links Gold‑Rush California to today’s economic and cultural dynamics

Nathaniel Philbrick’s ‘The Rush’ revisits the California Gold Rush, covering roughly 1848‑1860 and its aftermath, to show how a resource boom reshaped the American West. Philbrick weaves together the experiences of miners, entrepreneurs, Native peoples, and law‑enforcement officials, illustrating how violence, speculative finance, and rapid town‑building created patterns of boom‑and‑bust that echo in today’s tech and biotech clusters. He highlights specific episodes—such as the 1850 Sacramento flood and the 1856 Comstock Lode—that illustrate how infrastructure, migration, and state policy interacted. While praised for vivid storytelling, some critics note Philbrick’s occasional over‑emphasis on narrative drama at the expense of nuance. Nonetheless, the book underscores the lasting economic and cultural legacies of a resource‑driven surge, offering a historical lens for understanding contemporary entrepreneurial ecosystems and regulatory responses.

How the Columbian Exchange Reshaped Global Food, Animals, and Populations

The exchange altered diets, economies, and population growth worldwide, linking continents through crops, livestock, and microbes. Its effects appear in everyday foods, agricultural practices, and the demographic makeup of societies today.

1Definition and origins of the exchange

The Columbian exchange, also called the Columbian interchange, began after Christopher Columbus’s 1492 voyage and lasted from the late 15th century onward. It describes the massive movement of plants, animals, and microbes between the Americas (the New World) and Afro‑Eurasia (the Old World). Some transfers were intentional, such as bringing European livestock to the Americas, while others were accidental, like the spread of pathogens. The term was coined in 1972 by historian Alfred W. Crosby, who highlighted how these biological and cultural flows reshaped ecosystems and societies on both sides of the Atlantic.

2Transfer of American crops and their impact

American crops such as maize, potatoes, tomatoes, tobacco, cassava, sweet potatoes, and chili peppers traveled to the Old World and became staple foods worldwide. Potatoes, unknown outside South America before 1500, were widely cultivated in Europe by the 18th century and later supplied 12‑25 % of population growth in Afro‑Eurasia from 1700 to 1900, also driving urbanization. Maize and cassava spread to Africa and Asia, replacing traditional grains and boosting local populations. In Europe, the new crops diversified diets and increased nutrition, while in the Americas, Old World crops like rice, wheat, and sugarcane were introduced, reshaping agriculture and economies.

3Animal transfers and new uses

Most animal species moved from the Old World to the New, because Eurasia already domesticated many livestock. Horses, donkeys, mules, pigs, cattle, sheep, goats, chickens, dogs, cats, and bees were quickly adopted by Indigenous peoples for transport, food, and work. Plains Indians used horses for hunting, while African slaves taught rice cultivation techniques that relied on water control. In the opposite direction, the turkey and Muscovy duck traveled from the Americas to Europe, becoming common poultry. These animal transfers altered diets, labor systems, and military capabilities, exemplified by the Mapuche’s rapid adoption of horses that enhanced their resistance against Spanish colonizers.

4Disease exchange and demographic collapse

The exchange of pathogens proved far more lethal than that of plants or animals. Old World diseases such as smallpox, measles, whooping cough, chickenpox, bubonic plague, typhus, and malaria arrived in the Americas with European colonists and decimated Indigenous populations. Smallpox alone killed an estimated 40 % of the 200,000 residents of Tenochtitlan in 1520, and overall 80‑95 % of Native Americans died within the first 150 years after 1492. In contrast, the New World contributed syphilis to Europe and quinine from cinchona bark, the first effective malaria treatment. These disease flows reshaped demographic patterns, enabling European colonization and the trans‑Atlantic slave trade.

5Cultural and demographic transformations

The movement of people accompanied the biological exchange, creating profound cultural shifts. European settlers and African slaves replaced many Indigenous communities across the Americas; the number of Africans taken to the New World far exceeded European migrants in the first three centuries. Enslaved Africans brought agricultural knowledge, such as rice cultivation, that became central to Southern plantations. The spread of New World crops like chili peppers and potatoes transformed cuisines in Europe, Africa, and Asia, making dishes such as curry globally common. Likewise, Old World crops—rice, wheat, and sugarcane—restructured American agriculture, while the introduction of coffee and cacao created new commodity economies.

How digital silence reshapes grief and identity during wartime disconnection

“This piece captures how the collapse of digital infrastructure during conflict turns routine communication into a primary indicator of survival, reshaping how we grieve and maintain identity across borders.”

1The sudden loss of digital contact transforms routine communication into a fragile, life-or-death signal.

The author describes a decade of stable connection with parents in Iran via Telegram and video calls, which abruptly ceased on February 28 when war began. This shift turned daily rituals, like the 6 pm call, into memories of a vanished world. The narrative highlights how the 'last seen' status and disappearing names in group chats created a shared awareness of potential finality. The author notes that the silence was not just technical but existential, leaving them feeling 'homeless' and 'backless' in their daily life in India. The text illustrates that in crisis, digital presence becomes the primary metric for survival, where a single unknown phone call from the mother became a profound relief, confirming they were 'all alive.' This underscores the psychological weight of digital infrastructure in maintaining familial bonds across geopolitical conflicts.

2Grief manifests through digital artifacts, such as screenshots and archived chats, serving as tangible keepsakes of lost connection.

The author finds comfort in a screenshot of a cooking video liked by their mother before the disconnection, describing it as a 'yadgari' or keepsake. This object represents a 'trace of her touch pressed into a screen,' offering a tangible link to the past. The narrative details how the author initially avoided viewing their parents' photos, fearing the confirmation of their absence, but eventually returned to archived chats to read old comments and hear their father's voice. This behavior illustrates a specific form of digital mourning where the interface itself becomes a site of memory. The author mentions crying while reading diaspora comments, indicating that the digital space became a communal place for processing loss. The reliance on these static digital traces contrasts sharply with the dynamic, real-time interaction that previously defined their relationship, highlighting how technology mediates even the most intimate forms of grief.

3The disruption of digital normalcy alters daily behavior, creating a persistent state of alertness and social isolation.

Following the disconnection, the author’s routine in Bengaluru changed drastically. They began refreshing news channels and messaging apps constantly, even during conversations or while standing in a school playground. This compulsive checking behavior led to social withdrawal, as other parents learned not to approach them. The author describes moving through ordinary routines differently, with the 'shadow of war' lingering in the background. The text notes that while others treated the situation as news, for the author, it was a constant, inescapable presence. This behavioral shift demonstrates how the loss of digital connectivity can permeate physical spaces, altering one's engagement with the immediate environment. The author’s eventual acceptance of the silence, marked by a willingness to see old photos again, suggests a gradual adaptation to a new, precarious normalcy where connection is intermittent and uncertain.
