Biopharma venture tracking shows strong backing for clinical transitions and novel discovery platforms

“Venture capital is clearly coalescing around validated clinical translation and next-generation platforms that scale synthetic biology and mRNA delivery.”

1Mega-funding rounds prioritized clinical advancement for complex therapeutic modalities, led by OMass Therapeutics' $310 million Series C and Soufflé Therapeutics' $135 million round.

OMass Therapeutics led recent biopharma funding with a $310 million Series C round to advance its melanocortin-2 receptor antagonist, OMS1620, into Phase 2 development for congenital adrenal hyperplasia and Cushing’s syndrome. The Oxford-based biotech aims to establish itself as a major European biotech player. Meanwhile, Boston-based Soufflé Therapeutics raised $135 million in Series B financing—following a $200 million Series A—to move three oligonucleotide candidates into clinical testing, starting with facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy. Matchpoint Therapeutics also closed a $150 million Series B round to bring its lead oral T-cell modulator, MPT-062, into clinical trials for autoimmune and inflammatory diseases. Additionally, Marrow Therapeutics secured $120 million in Series B funding alongside a $54 million Series A to advance its in vivo transferrin antibody bioconjugate platform for blood disorders. Together, these raises demonstrate substantial venture backer confidence in advancing complex therapeutic modalities into Phase 1 and Phase 2 human trials.

2San Diego biotechs secured notable funding, highlighted by Seawolf Therapeutics' $100 million Series A and KymaThera's $80 million Series B raise.

San Diego biotechs secured significant venture capital and strategic partnerships across late 2025 fundraising rounds. Seawolf Therapeutics emerged from stealth with $100 million in Series A funding backed by Versant Ventures, Eli Lilly, Novartis Venture Fund, CRISPR Therapeutics, and Alexandria Venture Investments. Concurrently, Seawolf announced a multi-target research collaboration and exclusive license agreement with Sanofi worth up to $1.2 billion to develop undisclosed DNA therapeutics for bleeding and metabolic disorders. Another local firm, KymaThera, capped off October fundraising by securing an $80 million Series B round from investors including Alta Partners, Venrock, and Foresite Capital. KymaThera plans to channel the proceeds directly into initiating Phase 1 clinical trials by the end of the year for its lead candidate, K-1728, a PI3Kα inhibitor. These transactions highlight strong investment activity for southern California biopharma innovators across both early-stage gene therapy platforms and targeted oncology pipelines entering clinical evaluation.

3Investors poured funds into enabling platforms, targeting enzymatic DNA synthesis for AI discovery, specialized mRNA delivery systems, and vectorized antibody gene therapies.

Biopharma investors directed substantial late-stage venture rounds toward enabling platform technologies, including DNA synthesis and advanced delivery mechanisms. Ansa Biotechnologies closed a $71.5 million Series C round, bringing its total funding to $211 million. Ansa’s enzymatic DNA synthesis platform allows researchers to build long, complex synthetic DNA essential for AI-enabled drug discovery. Colorado-based VitriVax raised an $87 million Series C round to bring its Lyme disease vaccine into the clinic. VitriVax utilizes an atomic layering thermostable antigen and adjuvant (ALTA) platform designed to enable single-dose administration and improve mRNA payload delivery efficiency. In the gene therapy domain, Netherlands-based VectorY Therapeutics secured $83.5 million to advance vectorized antibody programs for ALS and Huntington’s disease. Additionally, Copenhagen spinout Commit Biologics raised $60 million in seed capital for bispecific complement engagers, while Ritzey Therapeutics secured $112.5 million to advance DYRK1A inhibitors acquired from Perha Pharmaceuticals for chronic metabolic and neurologic diseases.

US‑China Biotech Tensions: Walls Won’t Preserve America’s Lead, Investment Will

“The real risk to U.S. biotech leadership isn’t Chinese capital but our own dwindling research investment, so walls won’t help.”

1China has matched or exceeded the U.S. in new cell therapies and monoclonal antibodies.

Kin​ch notes that Chinese sponsors moved from about 30 experimental drugs in 2013 to over 400 a decade later, with a sharp focus on biologics. By the late 2010s Chinese firms were introducing more cell‑therapy candidates annually than their American counterparts, and by the early 2020s they had matched or surpassed the U.S. in the number of new monoclonal antibodies. In particular, China leads in multispecific antibodies and antibody‑drug conjugates. The U.S. still dominates nucleic‑acid drugs and gene‑therapy platforms, reflecting decades of public‑private investment in lipid nanoparticles, viral vectors and CRISPR. Kin​ch cautions that raw counts may overstate genuine breakthroughs, but the modality‑specific gap is evident in the Center for Research Innovation in Biotechnology’s analysis of 17,000 drugs entering human trials between 2000 and 2024.

2U.S. biotech investment restrictions risk harming domestic innovation more than protecting national security.

The Treasury Department proposal, announced in mid‑October, would bar American investors from equity stakes, joint ventures, or limited‑partner positions in Chinese biotech firms and their equipment suppliers, while leaving licensing of Chinese molecules untouched. Kin​ch argues that the primary threat to U.S. leadership is not Chinese capital but the erosion of the domestic research enterprise: American experimental‑drug output has been flat since 2018, NIH and other federal funding are shrinking, and universities that built the nation’s lead in nucleic‑acid and gene‑therapy research face budget cuts. Treating the entire biotech sector as a single target ignores the divergent competitive races across modalities, and a blanket ban could further depress the already stagnant pipeline without delivering any new medicines to patients.

3Three policy steps—targeted comment, focused funding, nuanced metrics—could outperform blanket bans.

Kin​ch recommends that stakeholders use the Treasury’s public comment period (open through Dec 7) to push for narrowly drafted restrictions aimed only at transfers of platforms and capabilities, not all investment, and to demand clear review dates and objective criteria for any rule. He also urges policymakers to prioritize stable funding for areas where the U.S. still leads—nucleic‑acid and gene‑therapy research—through mechanisms such as NIH, ARPA‑H and domestic manufacturing incentives. Finally, he suggests creating long‑term indicators that track first‑in‑human trials by drug type and sponsor country, rather than relying on aggregate annual counts, to better measure progress and guide future policy decisions.

Trump Visa Restrictions Chill International Biomedical Graduate Enrollment

1International graduate enrollment fell 9% overall, 15% among new students

Data released by the Institute of International Education from a survey of over 800 U.S. institutions show foreign student enrollment for the 2025‑2026 academic year is down 9% overall and 15% among newly enrolled graduate students. The decline follows a federal judge’s November 16 ruling blocking a proposed four‑year cap on duration of status for international students and postdocs, and a separate injunction preventing a narrowed work‑authorization program. Student advocates such as NAFSA CEO Fanta Aw hailed the court victories, but the enrollment numbers suggest a chilling effect despite the legal setbacks. The Department of Homeland Security disputed the IIE figures, claiming its own SEVIS data do not reflect a 9% drop, while an independent analysis reported a 6.3% decline between October 2025 and October 2026.

Vyriad’s quiet in vivo CAR-T platform attracts Regeneron and Novartis partnerships

“It’s interesting how Vyriad is proving that deep, quiet scientific rigor in in vivo delivery can outperform the usual biotech hype cycle when courting Big Pharma partners.”

1Vyriad secured major Big Pharma deals by leveraging its proprietary in vivo delivery technology rather than relying on public hype.

Despite describing itself as a "very quiet company" with low media visibility, Vyriad has attracted significant interest from major pharmaceutical partners. In 2019, Regeneron partnered with Vyriad to launch a phase 2 trial combining its PD-1 inhibitor Libtayo with Vyriad’s oncolytic virus Voyager-V1 for various cancers. More recently, in 2024, Novartis entered a pact to utilize Vyriad’s active targeting lentiviral vector platform for new in vivo CAR-T therapies. Luke Russell, Vyriad’s president, noted that while the company is rarely reported on, Big Pharma partners are "extremely impressed" with the team’s scientific depth and comprehensive problem-solving approach. This strategic quietude allows Vyriad to build strong working relationships, such as with Novartis, where they are making progress toward the clinic, demonstrating that substantive scientific capability can drive high-value collaborations even without a loud public profile.

2The company’s core innovation involves re-targeting VSV-G lentiviral vectors to deliver CAR-T genes directly inside the patient’s body.

Vyriad’s platform addresses a key limitation in traditional lentiviral vectors, which are typically used for ex vivo engineering of T cells. By introducing a mutation in the vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein G (VSV-G), Vyriad prevents the virus from binding its ubiquitous natural receptor and instead re-targets it to CD3-positive T-cells. This allows for in vivo gene delivery, programming T cells within the patient to attack specific antigens. The company has commercialized its G-link delivery technology, which uses a trimeric architecture for high-avidity attachment to VSV-G, making in vivo gene therapy more accessible to academic researchers and biotechs. This technical approach distinguishes Vyriad from competitors who may rely on less targeted delivery mechanisms, offering a precise method for activating endogenous immune cells without the need for complex ex vivo manufacturing processes.

3Vyriad is advancing VV169, a BCMA-targeting in vivo CAR-T therapy, into phase 1 trials for multiple myeloma.

The company’s lead oncology asset, VV169, is currently in a phase 1 trial for multiple myeloma. A unique feature of VV169 is the reverse orientation of the CAR construct within the viral genome. This design blinds the viral genome’s reader to the CAR sequence, preventing accidental expression of the CAR protein by the virus itself, which could otherwise cause adverse events like clonal relapses. While the broader biotech industry is increasingly pivoting CAR-T technologies toward autoimmune diseases, Vyriad is deliberately maintaining its focus on oncology. President Luke Russell explained that the risk-benefit profile for cancer patients is distinct from that of autoimmune patients, and the company believes that developing lower-dosing, more tolerable therapies is the necessary path to eventually unlocking the autoimmune population for in vivo lentiviral vectors. Early readouts from the VV169 trial are expected around late 2027.

Iran’s Revolutionary Regime Becomes More Resilient After 2026 War

“Iran’s paradox of a shrinking economy and a resilient revolutionary elite means the 2026 war is unlikely to produce lasting stability, but merely another pause in a long‑term confrontation.”

1War with the U.S. and Israel hardened Iran’s revolutionary elite rather than destabilizing it.

The article notes that in 2026 Iran survived a nationwide uprising and a devastating war launched jointly by the United States and Israel. The conflict eliminated many senior officials, crippled the conventional military, and devastated the economy, yet instead of prompting a reassessment of revolutionary policy, the ruling clerics used the crisis to reinforce their ideological narrative. After the death of Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, his son Mojtaba Khamenei was anointed as the new supreme leader, with chants of “God’s grace is clear” echoing in the streets. According to the author, the war acted as a rallying point for the revolutionary elite, consolidating their resolve around resistance rather than governance. This pattern mirrors earlier crises—such as the 1979 hostage seizure and the eight‑year Iran‑Iraq war—where external threats strengthened internal cohesion and entrenched theocratic rule.

2Iran’s economy continues to shrink while its population becomes more urban, educated, and socially liberal.

The piece contrasts Iran’s stagnant macro‑economy with rapid social change. Real GDP per capita remains roughly a quarter below its 1976 peak, indicating that most Iranians are materially poorer than their parents and grandparents. Inflation has eroded the currency and the country’s oil‑dependent revenues have been repeatedly disrupted by sanctions and war. At the same time, the population has nearly tripled, urbanization has accelerated, and education levels have risen, creating a more liberal and outward‑looking citizenry. Since the 2022‑23 “Woman, Life, Freedom” protests, mandatory veiling has become increasingly unenforceable, and women regularly appear in public without headscarves. The author argues that these social shifts undermine the regime’s traditional legitimacy, yet the clerical leadership continues to cling to revolutionary rhetoric, creating a widening gap between a populace that looks forward and rulers who look backward.

3The regime’s three durability pillars—elite cohesion, loyal coercive forces, and suppression of rivals—make political change unlikely.

The author cites political scientists Steven Levitsky and Lucan Way, who identify three pillars that allow revolutionary regimes to outlast their non‑revolutionary counterparts: a cohesive ruling elite, a highly developed loyal coercive apparatus, and the systematic destruction of rival institutions. Iran exemplifies all three. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, born out of the 1980‑85 Iran‑Iraq war, functions as both a military force and a political power broker, ensuring elite cohesion. The regime’s security services have repeatedly suppressed dissent, from the 1979 hostage crisis to the recent mass killings of protesters, reinforcing a loyal coercive network. Finally, independent political actors, reformist politicians, and civil‑society groups have been imprisoned, exiled, or eliminated, eroding alternative centers of power. Consequently, the article argues that the 2026 war is likely only an intermission; without a fundamental political rupture in Tehran, the status quo of confrontation and internal decay will persist.

Voters prioritize economic competence over democratic norms in recent elections

“The recent election results reflect voters reacting to economic competence rather than a sudden ideological shift toward defending democratic norms.”

1Exit polls overstate democratic motivation; economic grievances drive voting behavior more than abstract principles.

The author argues that while 2026 exit polls showed a third of voters citing 'the state of democracy' as their top issue, this reflects social desirability bias rather than true motivation. A YouGov open-ended poll found only five percent spontaneously cited corruption or democratic undermining as their primary driver. In contrast, 39 percent named the economy as the most important issue, with most voting for Democrats. This mirrors 2024, when 32 percent prioritized the economy and voted for Trump. The article cites Yale political scientists Matthew Graham and Milan Svolik, who found that only a small fraction of Americans prioritize democratic principles when it conflicts with partisan identification or policy preferences. The author contends that interpreting these results as a sudden surge in democratic guardianship misunderstands the electorate’s focus on leader competence and personal well-being.

2Global 'antiauthoritarian wave' is a mischaracterization of routine political pendulum swings driven by economic dissatisfaction.

The author dismisses the narrative of a global anti-authoritarian wave, citing Brazil, Hungary, and Serbia as examples where autocrats lost not due to principled resistance, but due to the 'law of the pendulum.' In Hungary, Viktor Orban’s defeat followed a decline in voter satisfaction with the economy and government, not a sudden shift in democratic values. European Social Survey data showed Orban’s supporters were not significantly more distrustful of democracy than other voters; they were simply fed up with the incumbent. Similarly, Lula’s narrow victory in Brazil was influenced by a specific debate moment rather than a broad democratic mandate. The author emphasizes that the fall of a strongman does not automatically produce a well-functioning democratic system, often leading instead to factionalism and gridlock.

3Democratic backsliding is durable; restoring institutions requires long-term struggle against entrenched executive power.

The author warns against expecting quick fixes to democratic erosion, using Poland as a cautionary tale where the 2023 change in government led to a constitutional crisis rather than immediate restoration of liberal democracy. In the U.S., the president retains significant power, citing the Supreme Court case Trump v. Slaughter, which upheld his right to fire FTC members. The appointment of Stephen Miller as chief of staff suggests continued conflict rather than cooperation. The article notes that while some policy changes can be rolled back, others, such as the purge of civil service experience and the expansion of ICE, are harder to undo. The author concludes that the next two years may involve intense factionalism, as the president and allies are far from powerless despite the midterm losses.

AI Accelerates Vulnerability Discovery, Raising Cybersecurity Challenges

1AI models now find thousands of new software flaws each month

Andrew Lohn notes that worldwide vulnerability reports jumped from roughly four thousand new flaws per month before April to about six thousand in the spring, and have since accelerated to sixteen thousand per month—a four‑fold increase. He attributes this surge largely to advanced AI models that can scan code and identify weaknesses far faster than human analysts. The rise is not merely incremental; the growth follows an exponential curve, suggesting that each additional compute boost yields disproportionately more discoveries. Lohn cautions that while AI can generate patches, the sheer volume strains existing security teams, increasing the chance of missed or faulty updates. The data underscores a fundamental shift: AI is turning vulnerability discovery into a high‑frequency, large‑scale process rather than occasional, isolated events.

2Patch race intensifies as AI‑generated fixes outpace reliable implementation

Lohn explains that the faster discovery cycle creates a race to patch, amplified by coordinated vulnerability disclosure practices. Traditionally, vendors have 45 to 90 days to fix a flaw after an ethical hacker reports it, but Anthropic recently cut the critical‑issue window to 30 days, arguing attackers are moving faster. This compression forces defenders to release patches at unprecedented speed, often before thorough testing, leading to secondary outages when patches break systems. Moreover, each new patch can itself introduce vulnerabilities, and the sheer volume—potentially ten times more than before—means reliability drops. The combination of rapid AI‑found bugs and rushed remediation creates a feedback loop where more fixes generate more failures, heightening operational risk across sectors from aviation to finance.

Democrats block permitting reform, stalling green energy and infrastructure projects

“The irony is that the same regulatory frameworks Democrats cite for environmental protection are actually the primary barrier to building the green infrastructure needed to meet climate goals.”

1The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) passed the Senate but faces likely failure in the House due to Democratic opposition.

The article argues that BAAJA is genuinely bipartisan and pro-affordability, yet Democrats supplied 31 of the 33 'no' votes in the Senate. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries has not indicated support, risking the bill's death. The author contends that while other affordability measures like tax increases or tariff removals involve painful trade-offs, permitting reform is a rare opportunity to lower costs without sacrifice. The bill aims to streamline infrastructure and energy project approvals, a goal supported by clean-energy manufacturers and the AFL-CIO, but blocked by a split Democratic caucus that cannot agree on its merits.

2The National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) has evolved from a review process into a litigation machine that halts critical infrastructure development.

Originally requiring short environmental-impact statements, NEPA has swelled into a legal hurdle demanding thousands of pages and years of delay. The author cites California’s high-speed rail project, which has spent billions over two decades with little to show, and notes that the U.S. has built only one new airport since the 1970s. Senator Martin Heinrich’s 17-year ordeal building a wind farm illustrates how 'environmental' review blocks even green projects. The article claims NEPA is not truly an environmental law but a regulatory barrier that freezes the built environment, preventing the rapid construction of solar, wind, and transmission lines necessary for decarbonization.

3Environmental groups like the Sierra Club and NRDC oppose BAAJA to preserve their litigation-based influence, despite claiming they want a better bill.

The article attributes opposition to organizations founded in the 1970s to preserve the status quo, such as the NRDC, which originally blocked a hydroelectric plant. These groups view the permitting process as 'democracy in its purest form' because it allows interminable lawsuits. A leaked conference call revealed that the goal was to stop the bill, not improve it, contradicting public claims that they seek a more progressive alternative. The author argues this tactic of delay mirrors how NEPA works, blocking both fossil-fuel and green energy projects, and that Democrats will blame themselves if these barriers remain when they next hold power.
