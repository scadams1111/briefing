U.S.-China biotech investment barriers miss the real threat: domestic R&D stagnation.

“Restricting outbound investment in Chinese biotech won't protect U.S. competitiveness if we keep underfunding domestic basic research while licensing their molecules to fill our pipelines.”

1CRIB data shows China leading in cell therapies and ADCs, while the U.S. maintains advantages in nucleic acid and gene editing platforms.

Analysis of 17,000 drugs entering clinical trials from 2000 to 2024 by Stony Brook professor Michael S. Kinch and CRIB colleagues demonstrates a major shift in global biopharma innovation. China expanded its yearly new clinical candidates from about 30 in 2013 to over 400 a decade later, reaching parity with total U.S. output, which has remained flat since 2018. This growth is heavily concentrated in key biologics: China now leads in novel cell therapies, monoclonals, multispecific antibodies, and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs). Conversely, the U.S. retains its lead in nucleic acid drugs and gene therapies, underpinned by decades of Western public and private investments in platforms like lipid nanoparticles, viral vectors, and CRISPR. Kinch notes that China's public sector and state-linked entities introduced over twice as many new clinical candidates in 2024 as their American academic and public counterparts, positioning Chinese institutions to capture long-term platform value.

2Proposed U.S. and Chinese investment walls fail to address cross-border licensing, risking a dynamic where U.S. firms merely license Chinese inventions.

Beijing recently proposed barring foreign ownership of domestic cell and gene platforms, while the U.S. Treasury Department proposed restricting American equity, joint venture, and limited-partner investments in Chinese biotech firms and suppliers. However, Kinch emphasizes that neither wall restricts licensing deals. Consequently, foreign licensing for Chinese biotech assets reached $120 billion through August, as American firms increasingly license Chinese molecules, run late-stage trials, and pay royalties rather than originating breakthrough candidates. Capital restrictions treat the diverse biopharma landscape as a monolith without stopping China's momentum in sectors where it already leads. To avoid an unproductive division of labor, Kinch urges Treasury to use its public comment period through Dec. 7 to narrow restrictions strictly to capability and platform transfers, establish objective criteria to review rule efficacy, and clarify regulations regarding new U.S. companies created with Chinese licensors.

3Sustaining American biopharma leadership requires reinvesting in NIH, ARPA-H, and domestic translational research rather than relying on defensive capital controls.

Defensive capital restrictions do not put new medicines into clinical development or fix the erosion of federal support for U.S. research universities. Kinch argues America's lead in RNA therapies and gene editing stems from sustained public and private research funding, not trade barriers. To maintain competitiveness, policymakers must prioritize stable funding for translational models through the National Institutes of Health, ARPA-H, and domestic biomanufacturing infrastructure. Washington must also evaluate why early-stage clinical trials are increasingly initiating in China, examining domestic regulatory and investment hurdles. Furthermore, federal agencies should implement nuanced, long-term tracking of first-in-human trials by disease, drug type, and sponsor country to make informed policy. Ultimately, cutting off scientific and economic engagement hurts both nations, and U.S. leadership depends on fueling its domestic discovery pipeline rather than constructing regulatory walls.

Physicists Pivot to Ultralight Dark Matter Experiments as WIMP Searches Fail to Yield Results

“With traditional WIMP searches hitting a wall, the dark matter hunt is turning into a masterclass in precision metrology, using everything from thorium nuclear clocks to Earth's ionosphere.”

1As traditional searches for Weakly Interacting Massive Particles yield null results, physicists are pivoting to ultralight dark matter candidates like the theoretical axion.

For decades, Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs) were the leading dark matter candidate, but ongoing searches have repeatedly returned null results. A September 2026 detection signal at the Lux-Zeplin experiment's liquid xenon detector is widely viewed with skepticism due to its unexpectedly high energy, which likely indicates background noise rather than a true discovery. Consequently, researchers are shifting focus to ultralight dark matter, particularly axions. Hypothesized in the 1970s, axions would not only account for dark matter but also resolve the strong CP problem by explaining why a neutron's internal quark charges are perfectly aligned. Because axions are estimated to be a trillionth to a billionth the mass of an electron, traditional particle detectors are ineffective. Physicists are now developing specialized haloscopes—hollow metallic cavities placed within strong magnetic fields to convert passing axions into detectable photons. Teams led by Reina Maruyama at Yale University are modifying these haloscopes with copper rod arrays to scan higher-mass ranges, while researchers at the German Electron Synchrotron are utilizing high-powered lasers in light-shining-through-a-wall experiments.

2Cutting-edge quantum instruments, including nuclear atomic clocks and atom interferometers, are being deployed to detect the subtle wave-like oscillations of ultralight dark matter.

Because ultralight dark matter particles act more like continuous waves than discrete particles, detection requires hyper-sensitive equipment capable of measuring microscopic physical disruptions. Thorsten Schumm's team at the Vienna University of Technology recently developed a nuclear atomic clock using the thorium-229 isotope. Unlike standard atomic clocks that track electron transitions, this clock uses ultraviolet lasers to excite the atomic nucleus itself, making it uniquely sensitive to environmental perturbations caused by passing dark matter fields. Simultaneously, physicists like Oliver Buchmueller are utilizing atom interferometers, which cool atom clouds near absolute zero into quantum superpositions to detect inconsistency in their wave states. A 100-meter-long atom interferometer is currently under construction at Fermilab, with another planned at CERN. Additionally, researchers at Leiden University are testing levitated dust-grain-sized magnets in superconducting vacuum traps, led by Dorian Amaral. If dark matter waves oscillate at the magnet's natural resonant frequency, the resulting amplified motion will provide measurable evidence of dark matter passing through Earth.

3Researchers are also analyzing Earth's geomagnetic field for dark matter signals while keeping alternative theories like primordial black holes under active consideration.

Beyond lab-based quantum hardware, astrophysicists are turning to macro-scale detectors, including Earth's ionosphere. A team in Japan led by Atsushi Taruya analyzed ten years of magnetometer data from Scotland, identifying 65 electromagnetic signals that match expected profiles for axionlike particles interacting with Earth's magnetic field. Confirming these findings requires cross-referencing global readings to account for local variations in the geomagnetic field, leading the team to analyze additional dataset records from Japan. Despite these diverse experimental upgrades, dark matter remains entirely unconfirmed, leaving open the possibility that current particle models are wrong. If ultralight dark matter searches fail like the WIMP experiments before them, theoretical physics may turn toward alternative explanations. These include Stephen Hawking's 1970s hypothesis that dark matter consists of primordial black holes created immediately following the Big Bang, or even more radical revisions to our fundamental understanding of cosmological gravity.

FDA's shifting cell and gene therapy stance sparks industry clash before recent reset

“The FDA's brief push for rigid placebo controls in ultra-rare gene therapies shows how fast agency leadership turnover can disrupt regulatory predictability for biotech pipelines.”

1The FDA briefly pivoted toward strict methodological rigor, rejecting rare disease gene therapies over trial designs using natural history controls.

According to Alliance for Regenerative Medicine (ARM) CEO Tim Hunt, an ideological shift occurred at the FDA that prioritized strict clinical trial methodology over flexible regulatory pathways for cell and gene therapies targeting rare, fatal conditions. The agency issued initial rejection signals for uniQure’s Huntington’s disease candidate, rejected Atara Biotherapeutics and Pierre Fabre’s Ebvallo treatment, and turned down Regenxbio’s Hunter syndrome therapy. For Regenxbio, the FDA demanded larger patient cohorts and an untreated placebo control arm, rejecting the company's reliance on natural history data—a standard industry practice given the ethical and practical difficulties of conducting placebo-controlled trials in ultra-rare pediatric populations. ARM engaged in intensive advocacy with senior White House, HHS, and Congressional staff, emphasizing that enforcing rigid methodological perfection is unfeasible for break-the-glass medicines. The conflict highlighted a growing tension between traditional regulatory demands for randomized controlled trials and the practical realities of developing single-arm gene therapy protocols for rare degenerative diseases lacking an established standard of care.

2Widespread leadership turnover across HHS and CBER exacerbated uncertainty for biotech developers before permanent appointments restored stability.

The regulatory turbulence occurred against a backdrop of severe administrative instability within the FDA and the Department of Health and Human Services. The agency operated without a permanent commissioner following the departure of Marty Makary, M.D., in May, a void lengthened when the White House pulled its nomination of Heidi Overton, M.D., Ph.D., hours before a Senate confirmation vote. Leadership within the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER)—which directly oversees biological products and gene therapies—was similarly volatile following the resignation of Peter Marks, M.D., Ph.D., in March 2025 and the subsequent 11-month tenure of Vinay Prasad, M.D. Industry confidence began to stabilize only after Karim Mikhail was officially confirmed as permanent CBER chief in September 2026, alongside key outreach efforts by acting FDA commissioner Kyle Diamantas. Mikhail introduced the Rare Disease Framework Initiative, aiming to create clearer, more predictable review pathways. This administrative stabilization has helped de-escalate tension between the agency, patient advocacy organizations, and trade groups representing cell and gene therapy developers.

3Recent FDA actions signal a return to pragmatic flexibility, restoring priority reviews and reversing rejections for key rare disease therapies.

ARM leadership notes that current FDA leadership has effectively corrected course, moving back toward a collaborative approach with biopharma developers and patient advocacy groups. This shift is demonstrated by concrete regulatory reversals: the FDA granted priority review to uniQure’s Huntington’s disease gene therapy ahead of a May 2027 decision date and reversed its stance on Regenxbio’s filing by agreeing to review the submission based on existing study data rather than requiring additional placebo-controlled trials. Additionally, executive engagement from acting commissioner Kyle Diamantas, who met directly with patient organizations, signaled a renewed commitment to inclusive communication. The newly established Rare Disease Framework Initiative is viewed by industry leaders as a crucial step toward establishing transparent criteria for ultra-rare disease approvals. While trade advocates caution that long-term execution remains to be evaluated, early feedback on the refreshed agency leadership is overwhelmingly favorable due to increased predictability and dialogue surrounding cell and gene therapy development.

AI CEOs Clash Over Whether Humans Should Cede Control to Superintelligence

“The clash shows that while some AI leaders see handing over critical decisions to superintelligence as inevitable, others fear it could trigger backlash and ethical fallout, shaping the future regulatory landscape.”

1Top AI executives publicly reject Anthropic’s push to hand over decision‑making to AI.

In recent comments, leaders from rival firms have pushed back against the view emerging inside Anthropic that humanity should surrender decision‑making authority to superintelligent systems. Meta’s head of AI, Alexandr Wang, posted on X that society should not accept a "handover" of control, citing the imminent superintelligence moment highlighted by Anthropic’s recent mathematics breakthrough. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman echoed this stance, stating he wants people to retain agency over the future and urging advocacy for human control. Dean Ball, OpenAI’s head of strategic futures, described the opposing view as an "extraordinarily common" perspective within AI‑safety circles, noting that many insiders are uneasy about the narrative of voluntary human abdication. These public rebukes contrast with internal Anthropic discussions that suggest a growing consensus among some employees that AI could safely assume authority in certain domains.

2Anthropic’s Dario Amodei argues AI should run life‑critical, defense, and education systems.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has repeatedly advocated for handing over decision‑making to AI in areas where machines could outperform humans. In a February New York Times interview he said that in "life‑critical, safety‑critical cases" society should turn authority over to AI, allowing time for adaptation. During a CBS discussion he suggested fully autonomous weapons might become necessary for defending democracy if adversaries deploy them first. He also projected that graduate‑student supervision could invert, with AI acting as the principal investigator and directing human researchers. In his 2024 essay "Machines of Loving Grace," Amodei outlined a speculative economy where superintelligent AI distributes resources based on derived human values, arguing that such delegation could be ethically justified if AI aligns with those values. These statements illustrate Anthropic’s internal vision of AI‑augmented governance across multiple sectors.

3Policy makers and industry leaders warn that surrendering agency could spark backlash and ethical crises.

Beyond corporate debate, political figures are voicing concern that ceding control to AI may inflame public and regulatory resistance. Maryland Governor Wes Moore, a potential 2028 Democratic presidential contender, invoked the Book of Genesis on "The Axios Show," asserting that God gave humanity dominion over Earth, not robots or agents. Elon Musk reiterated his long‑standing warning that superintelligence could reduce humans to "pet labradors," while simultaneously supporting brain‑computer‑interface technologies to preserve human advantage. These remarks reflect a broader anxiety that promoting voluntary human step‑aside could amplify societal fears, trigger stricter oversight, and complicate the rollout of advanced AI systems. The tension between visionary AI governance proposals and the need to maintain public trust underscores the emerging policy challenge surrounding superintelligence deployment.

Ambetter’s Prior‑Authorization Denials Outpace ACA Peers, Delaying Critical Care

“Ambetter’s unusually high prior‑authorization denial rates are forcing patients to fight insurers just to get standard cancer treatments, and the system barely lets anyone appeal.”

1Ambetter leads ACA marketplace in prior‑authorization denials, averaging 25% versus a 16% industry norm.

Only about 7% of Ambetter’s prior‑authorization denials were contested in the past year, yet more than half of those appeals succeeded, according to ProPublica. The low appeal rate reflects patient unfamiliarity with rights and the administrative burden of contesting decisions. When patients do appeal, persistence often yields reversal, highlighting a systemic inefficiency rather than a justified clinical gatekeeping. The disparity has spurred legislative action: Rep. Craig Goldman (R‑TX) introduced a bipartisan bill to require greater transparency on prior‑authorization use, and former state insurance heads have urged regulators to investigate Ambetter’s outlier status. These developments suggest growing policy pressure to curb opaque denial practices and protect patient access to necessary care.

China Leverages Trump‑Era Détente to Build a Higher Baseline Before Inevitable Competition

“China is locking in trade and diplomatic wins now to ensure a stronger hand when U.S.–China relations inevitably turn more competitive again.”

1Beijing is cementing trade and diplomatic gains from the Trump period before relations sour

The article notes that China is using the two‑year window of the second Trump administration to lock in tangible benefits. It is pursuing new trade deals, encouraging U.S. investment, and using public diplomacy to improve its image in America. Specific actions include seeking a higher floor on U.S. arms sales to Taiwan, applying pressure on regional allies such as Japan and the Philippines, and promoting exchanges that showcase Chinese economic strength. The strategy is described as a “higher baseline” that will remain even if future U.S. administrations revert to a more confrontational stance. Evidence of the approach includes the rapid reinstatement of NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang after Trump intervened, which Beijing interpreted as a coordinated U.S. signal. By securing these markers of progress now, Beijing hopes future policymakers will find it harder to roll back the gains, preserving a more favorable position in any renewed competition.

2Chinese strategists view the current calm as fleeting and expect a return to great‑power competition after 2028

According to the piece, many members of China’s policy community remain deeply pessimistic about the durability of the détente. They argue that the current calm is a product of Trump’s personal preferences rather than a structural shift, and they anticipate a return to “great‑power competition” once a new U.S. president takes office, likely after the 2028 election. The article cites China’s own narrative of “mutually assured disruption” following the 2025 tariff war and export‑control measures, but notes that Chinese analysts view these as temporary tactics. The authors also point out that Chinese strategists see the Democratic Party as less pragmatic and more ideologically driven, increasing the likelihood of a harsher stance toward Beijing. This pessimism underlies Beijing’s dual strategy: enjoy the short‑term benefits while preparing for an inevitable downturn in bilateral relations.

3Beijing is betting on positive framing and youth exchanges to win U.S. hearts, even as public opinion may swing back

The author explains that Beijing is betting on a positive framing of the relationship to win U.S. public support. Gallup polls cited in the article show favorable views of China rising from 15 % in February 2023 to 34 % in February 2026, a shift linked to Trump’s warmer policy. To cement this trend, Xi invited 100,000 young Americans to study in China over the next five years and has expanded people‑to‑people exchanges. The piece warns that if the next U.S. administration adopts a harder line, public opinion could harden again, and Beijing’s outreach might be perceived as manipulation. Nonetheless, Chinese officials argue that even a modest improvement in perception creates a higher baseline that can temper future competition and make any subsequent confrontations less damaging.

Vyriad’s Low‑Profile Strategy Secures Big‑Pharma Partnerships for In‑Vivo CAR‑T

“Vyriad shows that a quiet, science‑first approach can attract big‑pharma partners and advance in‑vivo CAR‑T therapies for cancer.”

1Vyriad leveraged quiet R&D to land Regeneron (2019) and Novartis (2024) collaborations.

Vyriad, founded in 2016 from Mayo Clinic spin‑outs Omnis Pharma and Magnis Therapeutics, has deliberately kept a low public profile while building a robust partnership portfolio. In 2019 the company secured its first major pharma collaborator, Regeneron, to co‑run a phase‑2 trial combining Regeneron’s PD‑1 inhibitor Libtayo with Vyriad’s oncolytic virus Voyager‑V1 across colorectal, head‑and‑neck and melanoma cancers. The partnership was managed by President Luke Russell, who emphasized alignment and issue resolution. Building on that success, Vyriad signed a 2024 agreement with Novartis to exploit its active‑targeting lentiviral vector platform for new in‑vivo CAR‑T therapies. Although Novartis has not yet acquired an in‑vivo‑focused biotech, the deal reflects confidence in Vyriad’s scientific depth. To date Vyriad has raised $85 million in Series B funding from investors including the Mayo Clinic, Regeneron and the Southeast Minnesota Capital Fund.

2Its lentiviral platform retargets VSV‑G to CD3‑positive T cells, enabling in‑vivo CAR‑T delivery.

Vyriad’s core technology modifies the vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein G (VSV‑G) so it no longer binds its native ubiquitous receptor but instead engages CD3‑positive T cells. This single‑mutation approach preserves VSV‑G’s fusion capability while redirecting viral entry, creating a dual‑function delivery vehicle. The company commercialized the G‑link system, a trimeric architecture that simultaneously engages all three VSV‑G protomers, delivering high‑avidity attachment to engineered lentiviral vectors and enabling selective transduction of T cells. G‑link is offered to academic labs and biotech firms to broaden access to in‑vivo gene delivery. In parallel, Vyriad’s lentiviral platform underpins its in‑vivo CAR‑T program, where the vector delivers genes encoding chimeric antigen receptors directly to patient T cells, bypassing ex‑vivo manufacturing and potentially reducing cost and complexity of cell therapy.

3Vyriad’s pipeline centers on oncology, with phase‑1 VV169 for multiple myeloma slated for 2027 readout.

Vyriad’s pipeline includes oncolytic viruses, CAR‑T therapies and the G‑link delivery platform, with a clear focus on oncology rather than autoimmune indications. The lead in‑vivo CAR‑T candidate, VV169, targets BCMA in multiple myeloma and features a reverse‑orientation CAR construct that prevents the viral genome from expressing the CAR protein, mitigating risks such as clonal relapse. A phase‑1 trial for VV169 is underway, with the first efficacy readout expected in late 2027. While many biotech firms are exploring CAR‑T for autoimmune disease, Vyriad’s leadership argues that the risk‑benefit calculus differs for cancer patients, and lower dosing strategies will be needed before expanding into non‑oncology populations. Russell expressed interest in further collaborations, noting the large potential of their delivery vehicles but acknowledging limited internal development capacity.

AI vulnerability discovery surge drives patching chaos, not necessarily attacks

“The recent tech outages aren't necessarily hacks, but the side effect of AI finding four times as many bugs as before, forcing defenders into a chaotic patching race.”

1AI has quadrupled monthly vulnerability discovery rates, overwhelming defenders and causing widespread outages.

Andrew Lohn of Georgetown’s Center for Security and Emerging Technology notes that new vulnerabilities jumped from roughly 4,000 per month in January to 16,000 by the following January, an exponential trend driven by AI models finding bugs more like humans do. This surge is the primary driver of recent outages at major entities like the FAA, Apple, Microsoft, and Chase. Lohn argues these are not necessarily cyberattacks but the result of defenders rushing to implement a massive volume of patches. Increasing patch frequency by ten times naturally increases the risk of patch-related system failures, creating a state of 'software chaos' where reliability suffers due to the speed of remediation rather than successful malicious intrusion.

2Open-source models and shortened disclosure windows have accelerated the race between attackers and defenders.

Lohn explains that guardrails on frontier models are bypassable, and open-source models from China are now months behind or equal in capability to closed frontier models. This democratization means sophisticated cyber capabilities are no longer limited to nation-states. Furthermore, coordinated vulnerability disclosure periods have shortened; Anthropic reduced the window for critical issues to thirty days to alert the public faster. This creates a 'race' where every disclosed vulnerability signals to attackers that a patch is needed, pressuring overwhelmed defenders to move quickly. The time lag between discovery and universal patching remains a critical vulnerability, as AI is better at finding flaws and writing patches than ensuring those patches are reliably implemented across global infrastructure.

3Physical resilience and economic disincentives mitigate the risk of catastrophic infrastructure failures.

Despite technical alarms, Lohn argues that actual catastrophic impacts remain limited due to physical resilience and attacker economics. For example, Iran-linked hackers accessed water supply systems but caused no serious damage because mechanical safeguards prevented dangerous actions regardless of digital inputs. Additionally, while ransomware attacks are up, payouts are down due to better backups, and health breaches affect fewer people overall. Lohn suggests that attackers often prefer less disruptive methods because cyberattacks lack the immediate 'buzz' of physical violence and carry high legal or escalation risks. The 'Cyber Pearl Harbor' scenario may be overwrought because existing defenses and the inherent costs of maintaining persistent threats limit the frequency of devastating strikes.

How M.F.K. Fisher’s grief fueled a culinary classic

“It’s a fascinating case study in how creative output can serve as a psychological coping mechanism for severe grief, turning personal tragedy into a cultural touchstone for culinary writing.”

1The book was written to distract from her husband’s agonizing, drug-dependent decline during WWII.

M.F.K. Fisher wrote 'Consider the Oyster' in 1941 while her husband, Dillwyn Parrish, lay in a drugged slumber after a leg amputation for gangrene. His pain was managed with 'analgeticum,' an opiate they had brought from Europe, but the war threatened to cut off the supply. Fisher used the book as a mental refuge, reminiscing about shared life in Swiss vineyards and French cafés to sustain them both. She described the writing process as a way to find nourishment in 'seemingly frivolous memories' while 'incendiary bombs fell.' The text serves as a meditation on pleasure as a survival mechanism, allowing Fisher to maintain a sense of well-being and connection despite the immediate reality of her partner’s suffering and the looming global conflict.

2Fisher’s literary career was catalyzed by a romantic and intellectual seduction of Parrish.

Fisher, who had struggled with rejection in her first marriage, found her voice through Dillwyn Parrish, a writer and artist from an affluent creative family. After meeting during the 1933 Long Beach earthquake, Fisher began writing witty meditations on food specifically to impress him. Parrish acted as her mentor, helping shepherd her first book, 'Serve It Forth,' into print by leveraging his sister’s connections at Harper & Brothers. Fisher explicitly stated she never wrote just for herself, comparing it to 'kissing yourself,' and instead wrote 'towards somebody I love.' This dynamic transformed her from a provincial California writer into a figure who infused American culinary writing with wit and voluptuous delight, establishing her as a major voice in gastronomy.

3The book’s light tone masks a tragic ending where Parrish died before seeing it published.

Although 'Consider the Oyster' is celebrated for its pleasurable and light tone, it was completed in April 1941, just months before Parrish’s death. He was suffering from Buerger’s disease, a rare condition causing inflammation and clotting in blood vessels, which threatened further amputations of his remaining limbs. Fisher described him as a 'small wracked man' and herself as 'condemned, plucked at by demons.' Parrish shot himself on August 6, 1941, meaning he never saw the book in print. Fisher’s decision to keep the text free of her personal tragedies reflects her role as a 'considerate host,' offering the reader a 'delicate, charming, nostalgic gesture' of pleasure even in the face of profound loss and despair.

Kodak Reports Fifth Straight Quarter of Growth, Driven by Advanced Materials Unit

“Kodak’s shift to analog shows how niche markets can drive profitability even in a digital age.”

1Kodak’s Advanced Materials & Chemicals unit leads revenue growth, up 21% YoY in Q3 2026

In Q3 2026, Kodak’s Advanced Materials & Chemicals (AM&C) division reported $99 million in revenue, a 21% increase over the same period in 2025. This unit now represents the company’s fastest‑growing and most profitable segment. In contrast, the Print division’s revenue fell 7% year‑over‑year, yet still contributed $165 million to the consolidated total. Overall, Kodak’s consolidated revenue for the quarter reached $276 million, up 3% from Q3 2025, while profits rose 7%. The company generated $27 million in cash during the quarter, underscoring operational strength. Executive Chairman and CEO Jim Continenza highlighted that AM&C’s performance is central to Kodak’s long‑term plan, with continued investment aimed at sustaining profitable growth through the year’s end.

2Hollywood’s renewed preference for film fuels Kodak’s motion picture business

Kodak’s motion‑picture segment has benefited from a resurgence in film usage by major Hollywood productions. Christopher Nolan’s IMAX epic "The Odyssey" employed over two million feet of Kodak film, using Vision3 250D 5207 for daylight scenes and Vision3 500T 5219 for low‑light and night shots. Cinematographer Hoyte van Hoytema praised the grain structure and versatility of these stocks, noting their suitability for extended exposure during magic hour. In 2026, seven films shot on Kodak were contenders for the Palme d’Or at Cannes, including titles such as "Paper Tiger," "Disclosure Day," and "Marty Supreme." These high‑profile projects underscore a growing industry preference for analog media, reinforcing Kodak’s strategic focus on its film portfolio.

3Kodak’s still film revival signals broader analog resurgence

Beyond motion picture stock, Kodak has re‑introduced a range of still‑film products, including larger‑format photo film stocks that were previously discontinued. The company has rebranded many existing stocks and unveiled new ones that echo classic formulations, positioning itself to capture the niche market of photographers and collectors. This move aligns with the broader analog revival trend, where demand for tangible media has increased among enthusiasts and professionals alike. By expanding its still‑film offerings, Kodak leverages its legacy expertise while diversifying revenue streams within the analog sector.
