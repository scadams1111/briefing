U.S. biotech edge eroding as China’s output accelerates

“The real risk to U.S. biotech isn’t Chinese capital but our own stagnant research pipeline, so nuanced policies matter more than blanket investment bans.”

1U.S. biotech leadership is eroding while China’s output surges

Michael S. Kinch notes that the Center for Research Innovation in Biotechnology tracked 17,000 drugs entering human trials from 2000‑2024. Their September 2024 Drug Discovery Today analysis shows China’s annual experimental‑drug introductions rose from about 30 in 2013 to over 400 a decade later, matching or exceeding U.S. numbers in cell therapies and monoclonal antibodies, especially multispecific antibodies and drug‑conjugates. By contrast, U.S. contributions have flat‑lined since 2018, and America retains a larger share only in nucleic‑acid drugs and gene therapies—areas built on decades of Western public and private investment such as lipid‑nanoparticle platforms and viral vectors. Kinch emphasizes that maintaining this lead will require renewed domestic support and strategic investment.

2Washington and Beijing are proposing investment walls that miss the nuanced competitive landscape

Kin​ch describes two parallel policy moves: on Oct. 16, 2024 Beijing announced a ban on foreign acquisition of Chinese cell‑and gene‑therapy platforms, while the U.S. Treasury, within a week, proposed prohibiting American investors from taking equity stakes, forming joint ventures, or making limited‑partner investments in Chinese biotech firms and their equipment suppliers. Both measures leave licensing untouched, so American companies can still license Chinese molecules and Chinese firms can sell products abroad. Kin​ch argues that these “walls” treat biotechnology as a monolith, ignoring that the U.S. and China compete in multiple, unevenly paced modalities. He points out that Chinese biotech deals reached $120 billion through August 2026, a one‑third increase year‑over‑year, suggesting that investment flows remain robust despite the proposed restrictions. The proposals also signal heightened geopolitical tension over strategic health assets.

3Targeted policy actions could preserve U.S. advantage better than broad restrictions

Kin​ch proposes three concrete actions to protect U.S. biotech leadership. First, he urges scientists and industry experts to submit comments during the Treasury’s public comment period ending Dec. 7, urging a narrow focus on platform transfers rather than blanket investment bans and clarity on “NewCo” arrangements where Chinese licensors take stakes in U.S. startups. Second, he calls for renewed, targeted funding for areas where America already leads—nucleic‑acid and gene‑therapy research—through stable support of NIH translational programs, ARPA‑H, and domestic manufacturing capacity, and for addressing domestic barriers that push early‑stage cell‑therapy trials to China. Third, he recommends that federal agencies develop nuanced metrics, tracking first‑in‑human trials by drug type and sponsor country, rather than relying on aggregate new‑drug counts, to better gauge long‑term health and economic impacts. These steps, he argues, would be more effective than broad “walls.”

New vaccine strategies elicit broadly neutralizing HIV antibodies in monkeys and humans

“The germline‑targeting approach is finally producing bnAb precursors in people, offering a realistic path to an HIV vaccine despite funding and drug‑competition hurdles.”

1Monkey studies show vaccination can elicit broadly neutralizing HIV antibodies

Three independent groups reported that sequential immunisation regimens in rhesus macaques triggered the production of broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) capable of neutralising diverse HIV strains. The studies, cited as references 2‑4 in the Nature article, used engineered envelope immunogens to prime and boost B‑cell responses, achieving antibody titres that prevented infection in challenge experiments. Rogier Sanders of Amsterdam UMC described the result as “a very important milestone,” noting that no prior vaccine had elicited such breadth in non‑human primates. While the work remains pre‑clinical, the data provide proof‑of‑concept that the germline‑targeting approach can shape the affinity‑matured antibody repertoire needed for protection, moving the field from a “shot in the dark” toward a defined immunological endpoint. These findings also align with earlier human studies that identified similar bnAb lineages, reinforcing the translational relevance of the primate model.

2Human trials of germline‑targeting immunogens generate bnAb precursors

Human volunteers receiving the engineered immunogen eOD‑GT8, developed by William Schief’s team at Scripps Research and now produced by Moderna, generated antibodies that match the genetic and structural hallmarks of bnAb precursors. In phase‑1 trials, nearly all participants produced these precursors after the priming dose, and a subsequent booster with a related immunogen induced additional somatic mutations that enhanced binding breadth and neutralisation potency. Schief, also vice‑president for protein design at Moderna, described the outcome as “worked beautifully,” highlighting that the approach can steer B‑cell evolution toward the desired epitope on the HIV envelope. Although the antibodies have not yet reached the full maturity of naturally occurring bnAbs, the data demonstrate that the germline‑targeting strategy is feasible in people and can be built upon for a full vaccine regimen.

3Funding cuts and prophylactic drugs complicate HIV vaccine development and testing

The article notes that the momentum toward an HIV vaccine coincides with two major external pressures. First, the U.S. government has sharply reduced funding for pre‑clinical and clinical HIV research, leaving several promising programmes on the brink of suspension, as highlighted by Dennis Burton of Scripps Research. Second, the recent approval of long‑acting prophylactic agents such as lenacapavir creates a high bar for vaccine efficacy trials, because demonstrating added benefit over an already effective drug will be difficult and may require larger, more expensive studies. Together, these financial and regulatory challenges could slow translation of the promising bnAb‑inducing strategies into licensure, underscoring the need for sustained public‑private investment and clear pathways for comparative efficacy testing.

Ambetter’s prior authorization denial rates exceed all other ACA marketplace plans

“The new federal data shows that Ambetter’s prior authorization denial rates are an outlier, effectively using administrative friction to deny care that would be approved if patients actually appealed.”

1Ambetter denied prior authorization requests at a 25% average rate, significantly higher than the 16% marketplace average.

ProPublica’s analysis of federal data reveals that Ambetter, the most popular ACA marketplace plan, has the highest denial rates in most states where it operates. In Texas, denial rates exceeded 32%, while in Iowa they reached 31%. The data, required for the first time this year, highlights a lack of transparency that has persisted since the marketplace launched in 2013. Former state insurance regulators described the outlier rates as "stunning," urging current regulators to examine the improper use of prior authorization. While insurers claim the process ensures cost-effective care, experts describe the decision-making mechanism as a "black box" that often blocks access to necessary treatments without clear justification.

2Only 7% of Ambetter denials are appealed, despite a majority of contested cases being overturned.

The article notes that while more than half of Ambetter customers who contest their denials succeed in getting coverage, the vast majority never attempt to appeal. Patients often fail to challenge decisions because they are unaware of their rights or find the process too complex and time-consuming. This dynamic allows insurers to maintain high initial denial rates, effectively using the administrative burden as a barrier to care. The low appeal rate suggests that the system relies on patient exhaustion rather than medical necessity to determine coverage, creating a significant gap between what is medically appropriate and what is actually paid for by the insurer.

3Denials caused critical delays in cancer and chronic disease treatments, often resolved only after political intervention.

Specific cases illustrate the human cost of these administrative hurdles. Richie Jones, a prostate cancer patient, faced an initial denial for proton therapy based on advice from an OB-GYN rather than a specialist; coverage was only granted after he contacted a U.S. senator and the state insurance commissioner. Similarly, Travis Christopher, who died of liver cancer, waited a month for CT scans after three denials, a delay that may have impacted his prognosis. Felicia, an obesity patient, had to contact the CEO directly after exhausting standard appeals. These examples demonstrate that access to care often depends on a patient’s ability to navigate bureaucratic systems or leverage political influence, rather than on clinical guidelines.

ARM CEO details FDA's shift from ideological rigor to pragmatic rare disease approvals

“The FDA’s recent rejection of rare disease gene therapies wasn’t just bureaucratic friction; it was a clash between ideological methodological rigor and the practical reality of treating deadly conditions where placebo controls are impossible.”

1FDA rejected several gene therapies in early 2026 due to new demands for control arms in ultra-rare diseases.

Tim Hunt, CEO of the Alliance for Regenerative Medicine, described a period where the FDA rejected candidates from Atara Biotherapeutics, Pierre Fabre, and Regenxbio. The agency’s rationale for rejecting Regenxbio’s Hunter syndrome therapy specifically required enrolling more patients and incorporating an untreated control arm, rather than relying on natural history data. Hunt noted these requirements were difficult for ultra-rare populations. This shift created friction, as ARM members reported conflicting experiences, with some facing intact review teams while others encountered significant regulatory hurdles. Hunt characterized this as a new ideology creeping into the FDA, prioritizing methodological rigor and 'perfect answers' over the urgent need for last-resort medicines for deadly conditions affecting children.

2Leadership turnover at FDA’s CBER and the commissioner’s office created instability before the current team corrected course.

The FDA experienced significant churn, remaining without a permanent commissioner since Marty Makary departed in May 2025. The nomination for Heidi Overton was pulled hours before a Senate vote. At the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER), Peter Marks resigned in March 2025, followed by an 11-month tenure by Vinay Prasad. It was not until September 2026 that Karim Mikhail was confirmed as permanent CBER chief. Hunt stated that Mikhail 'immediately steadied the ship' and adopted a practical approach. Additionally, acting commissioner Kyle Diamantas met with patient organizations soon after taking office in May, signaling a new day. These leadership changes coincided with a shift in regulatory posture, moving away from the earlier ideological focus toward more constructive engagement with industry and patient groups.

3Recent regulatory decisions show FDA returning to flexibility, including priority review for uniQure’s Huntington’s therapy.

Following ARM’s increased vocal advocacy and meetings with senior staff at HHS, the White House, and Congress, the FDA’s stance has softened. Hunt noted that the current leadership has 'corrected course,' resulting in favorable early reviews. Specific examples include uniQure’s Huntington’s disease gene therapy receiving priority review ahead of a May 2027 approval decision, and the FDA agreeing to review Regenxbio’s filing based on existing studies rather than demanding new trials. Hunt described the FDA’s new Rare Disease Framework Initiative as a 'very important initiative' aimed at making development more predictable. He emphasized that the current leadership is inclusive and communicative, allowing ARM to finish the year in a more comfortable position than when it began, though he cautioned to see how it plays out over time.

Iran’s Revolutionary Regime Gains Cohesion Despite War‑Induced Collapse

1War with the U.S. and Israel hardened Iran’s revolutionary elite, not popular support

The article notes that in 2026 a joint U.S.–Israeli war eliminated many of Iran’s senior commanders, crippled the conventional armed forces and plunged the economy into deeper recession. Yet, rather than prompting mass dissent, the conflict drove the ruling clerics to double‑down on revolutionary rhetoric. After the death of Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, his son Mojtaba was anointed as the new supreme leader, with chants of “God’s grace is clear” echoing in Tehran. The author argues that the war served as an external crisis that allowed the regime to close ranks, reinforce its ideological narrative, and marginalize moderate voices, confirming the pattern that revolutionary regimes become more cohesive after severe external shocks. The piece cites Steven Levitsky and Lucan Way’s research that such regimes last three times longer than non‑revolutionary ones, underscoring how the war amplified Iran’s internal durability rather than eroding it.

2Iran’s economy continues to regress while its society becomes more urban, educated and socially liberal

The author points out that despite five decades of revolutionary rule, Iran’s per‑capita GDP remains about 25 % below its 1976 peak, and inflation has repeatedly eroded the currency. At the same time, the population has nearly tripled, urbanisation has accelerated, and university enrolment rates are among the highest in the region. Social norms are shifting: since the 2022–23 “Woman, Life, Freedom” protests, mandatory veiling has become difficult to enforce and many women now appear in public without headscarves. These demographic and cultural changes suggest a society moving beyond the revolution, even as the regime clings to its anti‑American, anti‑Israeli stance, creating a widening gap between rulers and the ruled. The article notes that this socioeconomic divergence fuels internal pressure for reform, yet the regime’s reliance on the IRGC and a tightly controlled coercive apparatus limits any meaningful policy shift.

November biotech funding hits $1.1B, led by San Diego gene therapy giant

“The $1.2B Sanofi deal with San Diego’s Seawolf shows that gene therapy is no longer just a niche bet but a core strategic priority for Big Pharma.”

1San Diego-based Seawolf Therapeutics raised $100M Series A alongside a $1.2B Sanofi collaboration.

Seawolf Therapeutics, a San Diego gene therapy company founded in 2021, secured $100 million in Series A financing from investors including Eli Lilly, Novartis Venture Fund, and CRISPR Therapeutics. Simultaneously, the company disclosed a $1.2 billion deal with Sanofi involving a multi-target research collaboration and exclusive license agreement. This agreement focuses on developing undisclosed therapies for bleeding disorders and additional unnamed indications. The funding supports Seawolf’s DNA therapeutic ambitions and its lead program targeting metabolic diseases. This dual milestone—significant VC backing and a massive Big Pharma partnership—highlights the growing value of gene therapy platforms in the San Diego biotech ecosystem, positioning Seawolf as a major player in the region’s life sciences sector.

2Neuroscience and metabolic disease dominate November raises, with Ritzey and Delix securing over $100M each.

Ritzey Therapeutics, launched by former Intra-Cellular CEO Sharon Mates, Ph.D., raised $112.5 million in a Series A to advance DYRK1A inhibitors for chronic metabolic and neurologic diseases. The company acquired its clinical-stage, oral, once-daily candidate from France’s Perha Pharmaceuticals. Meanwhile, Boston-based Delix Therapeutics raised $100 million in a Series C backed by Johnson & Johnson’s venture arm to advance zalsupindole, a non-hallucinatory psychedelic therapy for depression. Delix plans to initiate a placebo-controlled phase 2b study in major depressive disorder in the first half of 2027. These raises reflect investor confidence in novel mechanisms for complex diseases, moving beyond traditional targets to address metabolic and psychiatric conditions with high unmet needs.

3Global expansion is evident as European and Chinese biotechs secure significant capital for clinical trials.

European companies captured substantial funding, including OMass Therapeutics (UK) raising $310 million for a melanocortin-2 receptor antagonist in adrenal disorders, and T Therapeutics (UK) raising $133 million for T-cell receptor CD3 bispecifics. In Asia, Hangzhou-based Eli Radiopharma raised 400 million yuan ($60 million) to advance its radiopharma pipeline toward clinical development. Additionally, Netherlands-based VectorY Therapeutics raised $83.5 million for vectorized antibody gene therapies for neurodegenerative diseases like ALS and Huntington’s. This geographic spread indicates that biotech innovation and capital flow are increasingly global, with strong activity in the UK, China, and the Netherlands, rather than being concentrated solely in the United States.

AI Accelerates Vulnerability Discovery, Amplifying Cyber Outages

“AI’s rapid vulnerability discovery is turning patching into a race that can cause more outages than the attacks themselves.”

1AI models quadruple monthly vulnerability discovery, reaching 16,000 per month

Andrew Lohn, senior fellow at Georgetown’s Center for Security and Emerging Technology, reports that vulnerability discovery has risen from ~4,000 per month in early 2023 to 16,000 in early 2024, a four‑fold increase. The trend began in April 2023, when new AI‑assisted scanners began identifying flaws in widely used languages like Java and PHP. Lohn cites graphs showing a steady exponential rise, suggesting that AI’s ability to emulate human‑like bug hunting removes the previous ceiling on discovery rates. This surge coincides with high‑profile outages at the FAA, Apple, Microsoft, and Chase, which were attributed to newly found flaws. The article notes that the sheer volume of vulnerabilities overwhelms defenders, as each patch can introduce new instability, leading to a cycle of outages.

2Defensive patching lags, causing more outages than attacks

The article explains that patching is a double‑edged sword: while AI can write patches faster, implementation reliability is lower. Each new patch carries a risk of breaking existing functionality, and with the number of patches increasing tenfold, the probability of outage rises proportionally. Lohn warns that defenders are already overwhelmed, and the rapid deployment of patches—sometimes within days—does not guarantee stability. The article cites that the patching process often leads to ‘patch‑related outages,’ and that the reliability of AI‑generated fixes is still uncertain, amplifying the operational burden on organizations.

3Open models and coordinated disclosure shorten vulnerability windows, increasing risk

Coordinated vulnerability disclosure practices have been shortened by AI labs. Anthropic, for example, reduced its critical‑issue window from 45 days to 30 days, arguing that attackers now find vulnerabilities faster. This acceleration means that the public is informed of weaknesses sooner, but also that attackers can exploit them before defenders patch. The article notes that the race between discovery and mitigation has intensified, with AI making it easier to find vulnerabilities but also making it harder to keep systems patched in time, thereby increasing overall risk.

2026’s Top Books Reveal Hidden Systems, Marginalized Histories, and Tech’s Societal Impact

1Environmental infrastructure gets literary spotlight in Nick Kawa’s 'After the Flush'.

Nick Kawa’s nonfiction work 'After the Flush' treats the humble toilet as a portal into the hidden world of sewage treatment. Drawing on a decade‑long experiment that placed a toilet seat over a bucket, Kawa documents how modern society’s “flush and forget” habit obscures the environmental and public‑health costs of waste. The narrative blends lyrical description with hard data on water usage, microbial load, and infrastructure strain, arguing that ignoring effluent pathways threatens both ecosystems and urban resilience. Kawa also peppers the text with cultural observations—such as the author’s occasional Wordle breaks while on the commode—to illustrate how everyday habits shape larger policy debates. The book’s emphasis on concrete engineering challenges and regulatory gaps makes it a timely reminder for anyone concerned with NIH/FDA‑type oversight of environmental health.

Tour du Mont Blanc mandates guides and reservations by 2027 to curb overtourism

“The new TMB charter is a case study in how destination management is shifting from open access to regulated, reservation-based models to balance tourism revenue with environmental preservation.”

1A new charter requires advance reservations for all trail nights and bans wild camping, replacing informal spots with paid, bookable bivouac areas.

Seven French towns along the Tour du Mont Blanc route have drafted a voluntary charter to manage the surge in hikers, which has increased by 50 percent since 2020, reaching roughly 80,000 people annually. The rules, aiming for implementation by spring 2027, mandate that every night on the trail be booked in advance. This system replaces the previous norm of informal wild camping, which has multiplied and caused significant environmental damage, including trail erosion and wildlife disturbance. Chamonix mayor François-Xavier Laffin summarizes the intent as "To reserve is to preserve," noting that similar quotas are being introduced at nearby Lac Blanc. While the charter covers only France for now and depends on local ordinances for enforcement, it signals the end of the era where hikers could simply show up and figure out logistics on the fly, shifting the burden of planning to a structured, reservation-based model.

2Operators can no longer sell self-guided packages; all groups must be led by guides licensed in France, Italy, and Switzerland, with group sizes capped at twelve.

The charter places licensed mountain professionals at the center of trail safety, prohibiting tour operators from selling self-guided packages that only include bookings and transfers. Instead, operators must include a guide certified in all three countries the trail crosses: France, Italy, and Switzerland. Daniel Rodrigues, general director of the Compagnie des Guides de Chamonix, describes this as a major step forward that ensures safety and creates economic value for local inhabitants. The rules also cap guided groups at twelve people. This regulatory shift addresses growing safety concerns, such as increased rockfall activity due to climate change and permafrost thaw, and the congestion that makes it difficult for hikers to navigate narrow ledges. By mandating professional leadership, the charter aims to mitigate risks associated with inexperienced hikers in increasingly hazardous and crowded alpine environments.

3Guides provide critical logistical advantages, including access to quiet trail variants and local accommodations, which self-guided hikers often miss due to lack of local knowledge.

The article highlights that the Tour du Mont Blanc is not a single trail but a network of paths and detours. Guides like Jason Day and Tanya Perret use deep local knowledge to select variants that avoid crowds and offer better experiences, such as the route toward Lac de Roselend. Perret, who has guided the trail over 75 times, notes that guides can quickly adapt to changing weather patterns and identify safe bypasses for dangerous sections, such as the ladders below Tête aux Vents. Additionally, guides manage complex logistics, including booking scarce accommodations like the ten-room Le Chalet de Roselend, which previously required in-person reservations. The charter also encourages operators to buy local and partner with local producers, supporting the regional micro-economy. For hikers, this means that while self-guided trips are still possible, the value of professional guidance increases significantly as the trail becomes more regulated and congested.
