    On a November day in 1994, a 14-year-old girl in Hong Kong named Charlene Hsu Chi-Ying collapsed on a busy sidewalk, in broad daylight, in her school uniform. She had starved herself to death. Charlene had never been diagnosed with anorexia, not because her self-starvation went unnoticed, but because the diagnosis, in its Western form, was mostly unknown in the East. Her death changed that overnight. Splashed across the front pages were stark headlines such as ‘Girl Who Died In Street Was A Walking Skeleton’. Local journalists scrambled for an explanation of this tragedy, and quickly found a template ready-made: a Western account of what anorexia was, what it felt like, who got it, what caused it. The ideas spread rapidly – particularly that anorexia involved distorted self-perceptions about one’s body (dysmorphia) and an intense, borderline delusional, fear of gaining weight, often cued by prevailing beauty standards (notably, a cultural fixation with slender female bodies). Mass public education campaigns followed. Hotlines opened, celebrities spoke out. Anorexia became part of the zeitgeist.
