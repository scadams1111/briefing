Good morning. It is Wednesday, November 25, and today we are looking at the shifting landscape of global biotech, the chilling effect on our academic pipelines, and why recent election results might not be the democratic triumph some believe them to be. 

We start with the biotech sector, where a new report from STAT suggests that the United States and China are both risking their own innovation by trying to wall off their industries. While Washington and Beijing are busy proposing investment restrictions, the real issue may be closer to home. Since 2018, U.S. drug development output has largely stagnated, while China has rapidly scaled its clinical trial contributions. In 2013, Chinese organizations introduced about 30 experimental drugs; by 2023, that number topped 400. Michael S. Kinch, the author of the report, argues that we should stop focusing on broad protectionist barriers and instead prioritize targeted domestic investment in academic discovery. He suggests that policymakers need to move away from simply counting drugs and start tracking first-in-human trials by specific disease categories to better understand where we are actually losing our edge.

Speaking of the future of our research, there is a growing crisis in our universities. A report from STAT highlights that international biomedical Ph.D. enrollment has dropped by about 30 percent since 2024. This is a significant contraction, especially since foreign talent has historically accounted for nearly one-quarter of all U.S. life science doctoral graduates. Even though federal judges have blocked some of the most restrictive visa policies, a chilling effect remains. Prospective students increasingly view the United States as an unwelcoming destination, and many are choosing to head to Asian universities instead. Experts warn that this shift is a structural threat to our long-term biomedical preeminence.

Turning to the global political stage, a piece in Foreign Affairs challenges the idea that recent election swings represent a principled defense of democracy. The author argues that we are misreading the electorate. While voters often cite the state of democracy as a concern in exit polls, open-ended data suggests that economic grievances are the true driver of change. The article also notes that the removal of an autocratic leader does not guarantee a stable democracy; instead, it often leads to gridlock and institutional decay. Perhaps most concerning is the generational divide: a 2025 Kettering Foundation survey found that while 89 percent of the Silent Generation believe democracy is the best form of government, only 54 percent of Gen Z respondents feel the same way.

In the defense sector, ProPublica reports that the Pentagon is considering a massive 15 billion dollar loan for Hadrian Automation, a startup backed by Donald Trump Jr.’s venture firm. This deal would be one of the largest in U.S. history, and it is raising eyebrows among former defense officials. While the Pentagon says the talks are in the early stages, critics worry that using the Office of Strategic Capital to fund companies with direct ties to administration insiders could jeopardize the unit’s credibility and future funding.

Meanwhile, the artificial intelligence industry is grappling with a deep philosophical divide. As reported by Axios, leaders are clashing over whether humanity should eventually cede control to superintelligent systems. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has suggested that in life-critical scenarios, it might be unethical to keep humans in the loop if AI can perform better. However, other leaders like Sam Altman and Alexandr Wang are pushing back, emphasizing the necessity of human agency. It is a shift from debating machine safety to debating whether we should voluntarily step aside.

On the engineering front, a commentary on iankduncan.com argues that AI is not inherently making engineers lazy, but it is being used by management as a pretext to strip away the rigor that keeps complex systems from failing. The author notes that recent infrastructure outages are often the result of decades of technical debt, and blaming AI slop is a convenient way for leadership to avoid accountability. In a similar vein, a developer using Lean 4 recently uncovered 17 previously unreported bugs in the Clojure programming language, proving that even in mature systems, formal verification can find logic errors that traditional testing misses.

In the Middle East, Axios reports that the White House is pressuring Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu to implement a 15-point Gaza peace roadmap. The plan includes disarming Hamas and transitioning control to a new Palestinian technocratic government. Despite the pressure, the plan remains stalled, and it is unclear if it is a genuine policy goal or just a political bargaining chip.

Finally, The New Yorker explores how AI is accelerating the discovery of software vulnerabilities. While AI helps find bugs, it also forces a rapid, error-prone patching cycle that likely contributes to recent tech outages. The author suggests that we may need to move toward formal methods—mathematically proving code security—rather than relying on reactive patching.

Today’s deep dive is on the resource curse. This is the paradox where countries rich in natural resources often struggle with economic growth and political stability. It is not an inevitable fate, but it often happens because resource booms can lead to Dutch disease, where a surge in exports makes other industries less competitive. Furthermore, extreme revenue volatility can destabilize government planning, and resource wealth often discourages leaders from investing in education or democratic reforms because they do not need to rely on tax revenue from a productive populace.

Anthropic’s potential initial public offering could create a tougher funding environment for smaller tech startups. There is internal management turmoil at the Africa CDC during the ongoing Ebola crisis. Democratic states are suing the Treasury over the rollback of corporate ownership reporting rules. And finally, the current measles crisis suggests a deeper, delayed societal reaction to pandemic-era policies.
